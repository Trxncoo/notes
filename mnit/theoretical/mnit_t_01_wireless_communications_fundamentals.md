## 1. Key Concepts

Wireless communication systems are the base of user and network mobility. Wireless transmission can take several forms, the most common being infrared transmission, radio-based transmission and laser transmission.

Going wireless is a trade-off rather than a free upgrade. It reduces the amount of infrastructure needed and its cost, allows faster network deployment, and is what makes user and network mobility possible in the first place, covering every geographic scope from personal and local area networks up to wide area and satellite networks. In exchange, wireless links have a much higher bit error rate than wired ones (as high as 10⁻³, against a typical 10⁻¹⁰ for wired links, though these numbers vary a lot depending on the specific technology and conditions), mostly because of noise, physical obstructions, multipath propagation and interference. Wireless channels also tend to be less secure, since anyone within range can listen in, and using them may require licensing or otherwise complying with spectrum regulations.

## 2. Terminology

An **electromagnetic wave** is generated by an electromagnetic field, with electric fields caused by the movement of electric charges. These waves propagate through several media, including the vacuum, and their speed, wavelength and frequency are related by `c = λf` (speed of light equals wavelength times frequency).

A **transmission band** is one of the conventional ranges the electromagnetic spectrum is split into, such as radio waves, microwaves, infrared or visible light. As a rule, lower frequency bands penetrate obstacles better and travel further, but carry less bandwidth than higher frequency ones.

**Free-space path loss** is the attenuation a signal suffers as a function of the distance between transmitter and receiver: received power falls off with the square of that distance, though other factors besides distance affect it too.

The **Doppler effect** is a shift in the received frequency caused by relative movement between transmitter and receiver, the same phenomenon that changes the pitch of a passing ambulance siren.

**Multipath propagation** happens when a signal reaches the receiver over several different path lengths at once, because of reflection, diffraction and scattering along the way. It causes **inter-symbol interference (ISI)**, energy from previously sent symbols still lingering while the receiver is trying to detect the current one, as well as fluctuations in the received signal's power. It isn't purely a problem, though: a receiver can sometimes make good use of the extra copies of the signal it provides.

**Modulation** is the process of using a signal to change some property, amplitude, frequency or phase, of a carrier wave, so that the signal can be transmitted over it.

The **baud rate** is how many symbols a system transmits per second, while the **bit rate** is how many bits it transmits per second. With M possible symbols, each one carries log₂M bits, so the bit rate is the baud rate multiplied by log₂M.

**Multiple access** techniques let several nodes share the same wireless medium, since that medium is a limited resource that needs to be used efficiently. The three main ones are **FDMA**, **TDMA** and **CDMA**, which split access between users by frequency, by time and by code, respectively.

A **cell** is the geographic area covered by a **base station (BS)**. Neighboring cells are given different frequency channels, but cells that are far enough apart can be given the same ones, a practice called **frequency reuse** that improves how efficiently the spectrum is used and raises the network's overall capacity.

**Handoff** (or **handover**) is the process of switching the base station serving a mobile terminal during an ongoing call, triggered by the terminal moving into another cell, by a drop in the quality of its current connection, or both.

## 3. Electromagnetic Spectrum

Electromagnetic waves were predicted by James Maxwell in 1865, observed experimentally by Heinrich Hertz in 1887, and first put to practical use for transatlantic radio transmission by Guglielmo Marconi in the early 1900s. A wave has an amplitude (its strength) and a wavelength (the distance between two peaks), and travels in a given direction:

```
   amplitude
      ^        wavelength
      |       <---------->
      |      .            .            .
      |     . \          . \          . \
------+----.---\--------.---\--------.---\----> direction of travel
      |   .     \      .     \      .     \
      |  .        \.  .        \.  .
      |
```

Its speed, wavelength and frequency are related by `c = λf` (speed of light equals wavelength times frequency). **Wavelength** is the physical distance between two consecutive peaks of the wave, while **frequency** is how many complete cycles pass a fixed point per second (measured in Hertz). Since `c` is constant, the two are inversely related: a higher frequency (more cycles per second) forces a shorter wavelength (peaks closer together), and a lower frequency (fewer cycles per second) allows a longer wavelength (peaks further apart).

By convention, the electromagnetic spectrum is organized into transmission bands, ordered from the lowest to the highest frequency. A **band** is just a continuous range of frequencies grouped under one label, because those frequencies tend to behave similarly (in propagation, penetration, available bandwidth) and get used for similar purposes; instead of referring to "300 MHz to 3 GHz" every time, it's shorthand to just say "UHF". Bands are also the unit regulators use to allocate spectrum, e.g. "the 2.4 GHz band is unlicensed".

```
 Hz    10^6      10^9  10^12       10^15  10^16 10^17            10^21
       |          |      |           |      |    |                 |
   Radio waves | Micro- | Infrared |Visible| UV  | X-rays | Gamma rays
               | waves  |          |light  |     |        |
       <---------------------------------------------------------->
       long range, low bandwidth              short range, high bandwidth
```

As frequency goes up, the wavelength gets shorter, and, as a rule of thumb, lower frequencies penetrate obstacles better and travel further, while higher frequencies offer more bandwidth but cover shorter distances and are blocked more easily. This comes down to energy and wave behavior: longer (lower-frequency) waves diffract around obstacles more easily and lose less energy over distance, while shorter (higher-frequency) waves carry more energy per cycle, which is what gives them more room to encode data (bandwidth), but also makes them absorbed or blocked more easily.

**Diffraction** is a wave's tendency to bend around edges and obstacles instead of being stopped by them, and it only happens significantly when the obstacle's size is comparable to (or smaller than) the wave's wavelength. Lower frequencies have longer wavelengths, so ordinary obstacles (walls, furniture, terrain) are small relative to the wave, which bends around them almost as if they weren't there. Higher frequencies have much shorter wavelengths, so the same obstacles look large by comparison, and the wave mostly gets absorbed or reflected instead of diffracting around it. That's why, for example, 5 GHz Wi-Fi (shorter wavelength) is blocked by walls more than 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi (longer wavelength), and why AM radio (very long wavelength) can travel around hills and buildings that would stop a microwave signal entirely.

That trade-off also shows up within the radio and microwave bands themselves:

- **Radio waves**, the lowest part of the spectrum: lower frequencies have long range but low bandwidth, higher frequency bands within the same range trade range for bandwidth.
- **Microwaves**, a high-frequency radio band: attenuated by anything in their path, so typically used for line-of-sight (LoS) communication.
- **Infrared**, just below visible light: high attenuation, so mostly used for low-range communication, typically indoors.

Spectrum is a limited, shared resource, so it has to be regulated to keep interference in check. This is done at both the international level, by bodies such as the ITU (International Telecommunications Union) and ETSI (European Telecommunications Standards Institute), and at the national level, by regulators such as the FCC in the United States or ANACOM in Portugal. Regulation covers several concerns: some bands are reserved for military, emergency and other public-safety use; others are reserved for commercial use and assigned through public auctions or other political and commercial policies; and some are left unlicensed, such as the 2.4 GHz band used by Wi-Fi and Bluetooth. Regulators also cap maximum antenna power, and still have to deal with open issues such as frequency pollution, reservation policies that differ from country to country, and the possible future use of "whitespace" radios.

## 4. Propagation

**Free-space path loss** is the attenuation a signal suffers purely from spreading out over distance, with no obstacles involved:

```
                r
   Transmitter -------------------> Receiver
```

The received power is proportional to `r⁻²` (the inverse square of the distance): double the distance between transmitter and receiver, and the received power drops to a quarter. This inverse-square relationship comes from the signal's energy spreading out over an ever-larger sphere as it travels, so any fixed-size receiver antenna only intercepts a shrinking fraction of it. Distance isn't the only thing that affects received power, though — obstacles, atmospheric conditions and the factors below also play a part.

The **Doppler effect** is caused by relative movement between transmitter and receiver, and it shifts the frequency the receiver perceives, the same way an ambulance siren sounds higher-pitched while approaching and lower-pitched while moving away:

```
 Moving towards Rx:            Moving away from Rx:

   Tx ----> ... ----> Rx         Rx <---- ... <---- Tx
   (received frequency           (received frequency
    appears higher)               appears lower)
```

**Multipath propagation** happens when the same signal reaches the receiver over several different path lengths, because of reflection, diffraction and scattering:

```
   reflection:              diffraction:              scattering:
   (bounces off a           (bends around             (bounces off many
    flat surface)            an edge)                   small objects)

   Tx        Rx              Tx                         Tx
    \        /                 \                        / \  \
     \      /                   \___                   /   \  \    Rx
      \    /                        \                 /     \  \  /
  -----\--/-----surface---      -----\----edge---   particles (rough
        \/                           \___ Rx          surface, scatters
                                                        in many directions)
```

Because each copy of the signal travels a different distance, they arrive at slightly different times and phases. This causes **inter-symbol interference (ISI)**, where energy from a previous symbol is still arriving while the receiver is trying to detect the current one, and it also makes the received signal's power fluctuate as the different copies add up constructively or destructively. Multipath isn't purely bad, though: it's part of what lets a receiver pick up a signal even without a direct line of sight to the transmitter, since energy still reaches it via reflections.

**Line-of-sight (LoS) communication** needs more than just an unobstructed straight line between transmitter and receiver: there's also a **Fresnel zone**, an elliptical region around that direct line, and objects intruding into it can still degrade the signal even without blocking the line itself. Some technologies, like laser links, don't suffer from this at all, but bring their own set of problems (e.g. needing a much more precise alignment).

All of this — distance, obstacles, multipath — ultimately affects the signal-to-noise ratio the receiver ends up with, and that ratio limits how much data can actually be sent. This relationship is captured by **Shannon's formula**:

```
W = H log2(1 + S/N)
```

where `W` is the channel's capacity (the maximum achievable bit rate), `H` is the channel's bandwidth in Hertz, and `S/N` is the (linear) signal-to-noise power ratio. The higher the signal-to-noise ratio, the higher the potential bit rate — intuitively, a cleaner signal lets the receiver reliably tell apart more distinct signal levels per symbol, which means more bits per symbol.

A **symbol** is one "unit" actually sent over the channel: a single pulse of the carrier wave, held at a specific amplitude, frequency and/or phase for a fixed slice of time. It's the physical thing the receiver measures, as opposed to a bit, which is just an abstract 0 or 1. If a symbol can only take one of 2 possible states (e.g. "high" or "low"), it carries exactly 1 bit. But if the transmitter and receiver agree on more distinguishable states per symbol (say, 4 different amplitude levels), each symbol can carry log₂(4) = 2 bits instead, without sending more symbols per second — that's exactly what digital modulation techniques like ASK, FSK and PSK exploit (see section 5), and why a cleaner signal (higher S/N) helps: it's easier to reliably tell apart many close signal levels when there's less noise blurring them together.

## 5. Data Transmission

Early radio communication systems used **analog transmission**, where the signal takes continuous values in time: it can be anything between a minimum and a maximum, with no fixed set of allowed levels. That's a problem once noise gets involved, because noise also takes continuous values and adds directly onto the signal, so once the two are mixed it becomes difficult, sometimes impossible, to tell them apart and recover the original signal cleanly.

**Digital transmission** avoids this by using a signal that can only take a finite set of values, changing between levels only at specific points in time. Because the receiver only has to decide which of a few known levels it's looking at (rather than reconstruct an arbitrary continuous value), it can tolerate a fair amount of noise and, in many cases, eliminate its effect on the recovered signal entirely. Digital signals also make it easier to use the spectrum efficiently, since compression techniques can be applied to reduce how much needs to be sent in the first place. A typical example is voice coding: the original analog signal strength is measured and rounded to the nearest one of a fixed number of discrete levels (e.g. 16 levels, encoded with 4 bits each), turning a continuous waveform into a sequence of bits.

### Modulation

Sending a raw digital or analog signal over the air isn't practical on its own, so it's used to **modulate** (change some property of) a separate **carrier wave**, a signal at a fixed base frequency chosen to travel well over the medium in use.

With **analog modulation**, an analog signal `x(t)` modulates the carrier. Two classic examples:

- **Amplitude Modulation (AM):** `x(t)` is added into the carrier's amplitude, `s(t) = (1 + x(t)) cos(2πft)`, so the carrier gets louder or quieter following the original signal.
- **Frequency Modulation (FM):** `x(t)` alters the carrier's frequency instead, `s(t) = A(cos2πf + ∫x(t)dt)`. Because the information rides on frequency changes rather than amplitude changes, FM is more resistant to noise than AM, since noise mostly disturbs amplitude.

With **digital modulation**, a bit stream changes the carrier at discrete moments in time instead of continuously. The three basic techniques each vary one property of the carrier to represent a binary symbol:

- **Amplitude Shift Keying (ASK):** binary 1 is sent as a waveform with amplitude A1, binary 0 as a waveform with amplitude A2 (fixed frequency throughout). This is exactly the same idea as AM above, just with only two allowed amplitude levels instead of a continuously varying one.
- **Frequency Shift Keying (FSK):** binary 1 is sent as a waveform with frequency f1, binary 0 as a waveform with frequency f2 (fixed amplitude throughout). Same idea as FM, but switching between exactly two frequencies.
- **Phase Shift Keying (PSK):** binary 1 is sent as a waveform with phase 0, binary 0 as a waveform with phase π (fixed amplitude and frequency throughout). A phase of π is just the same wave flipped upside down — every point that used to be above the axis is now below it at the same instant, and vice versa — so it isn't a change in how tall or how fast the wave is, only in when it "starts" its cycle.

Each of these can also be extended beyond a single bit per symbol: if there are M distinguishable amplitudes, frequencies or phases available, log₂M bits can be packed into a single symbol instead of just 1 (see the definition of a symbol in section 4). Combining variations in amplitude, frequency and phase at once lets even more bits be packed into each symbol, which is how real modulation schemes push more data through the same channel.

### Baud Rate, Bit Rate and the Nyquist Theorem

The **baud rate** is how many symbols are sent per second; the **bit rate** is how many bits are sent per second. With M possible symbols, bit rate = baud rate × log₂M.

For a channel with bandwidth B, using M different symbols, the maximum achievable bit rate is given by the **Nyquist theorem**:

```
C = 2B log2(M)
```

This is the bound for a perfectly noiseless channel — it only accounts for how fast the carrier can physically switch between M distinguishable states, not for any noise corrupting the signal. In any real channel, the actual achievable rate can never exceed **Shannon's limit** (section 4), which does account for noise via the signal-to-noise ratio. So Nyquist says how many symbols per second and how many states per symbol the channel could physically support, while Shannon says how many of those states can actually be told apart reliably once noise is factored in; the real ceiling is whichever of the two is more restrictive.

### Multiple Access

Multiple access techniques let several nodes share the same wireless medium, which is a limited resource whose use has to be maximized. There are three main techniques:

**Frequency Division Multiple Access (FDMA)** splits the available bandwidth into separate frequency channels, one per user or purpose. In interactive systems like cellular networks, channels are typically allocated in pairs: an **uplink** (from the user to the base station) and a **downlink** (from the base station to the user), with the uplink usually at a lower frequency than the downlink. Uplink and downlink may or may not get similar bandwidth — equal bandwidth in both directions suits telephony (where both sides talk roughly the same amount), but not traditional, asymmetric Internet access (where downlink traffic is much heavier), though this is less clear-cut with how the Internet is used nowadays. Guard bands, small gaps of unused spectrum, have to sit between channels to prevent inter-channel interference, at the cost of lowering how much of the available spectrum can actually be used.

**Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA)** instead splits a channel into time slots, letting different users transmit in turn over the same frequency. It comes in two flavors depending on how uplink and downlink are separated:

- **FDD-TDMA** (Frequency-division Duplexing): uplink and downlink each get their own frequency band, and each band is further divided into time slots.
- **TDD-TDMA** (Time-division Duplexing): uplink and downlink share the same frequency band, and are separated only by which time slots they use.

**Code Division Multiple Access (CDMA)** takes a different approach, rooted in the spread-spectrum technique (specifically Direct Sequence Spread Spectrum): instead of separating users by frequency or time, the transmission is spread over a much larger amount of bandwidth than it strictly needs, which incidentally makes it resistant to multipath interference. Every node is assigned its own n-bit code, and all nodes can transmit at the same time, over the same frequencies; the receiver uses each node's code to pick that node's traffic back out of the combined signal. It's used in all CDMA-based cellular systems.

## 6. Cellular Networks

### Concept

Cellular networks were introduced in the 1960s by AT&T Bell Labs to solve a basic problem: a single, powerful base station can only serve so many users before it runs out of spectrum, no matter how much power it uses. The solution was to split coverage into many smaller areas called **cells**, each served by its own **Base Station (BS)**, and reuse the available frequency channels across cells that are far enough apart not to interfere with each other. Neighbouring cells always use different channels, but a cell several "rings" away can reuse the same ones — this is called **frequency reuse**, and it's what lets a cellular network serve far more users overall than a single station could, since the same limited spectrum is effectively being used many times over across the coverage area, not just once.

A common way to visualize this is a repeating pattern of cells, each labeled with which frequency group it uses, so that cells sharing a label are always kept apart:

```
        1   1
      2   2
    3   3   3
      1   1
    2   2
```

Here, every "1" cell is far enough from every other "1" cell that they can reuse the same channels without stepping on each other, and likewise for "2" and "3". The tighter this reuse pattern (the fewer distinct groups needed before repeating), the more capacity the network squeezes out of a fixed amount of spectrum.

### System Architecture

A cellular network has three main components. The **Mobile Station (MS)** is the mobile terminal (the phone or device). The **Base Station (BS)** manages radio communication with all the mobile users currently in its cell. The **Mobile Switching Centre (MSC)** sits above a group of base stations, controlling all of them and interfacing the cellular system with the core network (and, from there, with other networks like the fixed telephone network or the Internet). This MS/BS/MSC terminology comes from 2G systems and isn't widely used verbatim anymore — later generations introduced different names for these roles — but the underlying concepts (a mobile terminal, something managing a cell's radio, and something coordinating multiple cells) still hold.

```
                         Core Network
                              |
              +---------------+---------------+
              |                               |
            MSC 1                           MSC K
              |                               |
       +------+------+                 +------+------+
       |             |                 |             |
       BS            BS                BS            BS
       |             |                 |             |
     Cell 1        Cell 2           Cell N-1        Cell N
    MS  MS         MS  MS            MS  MS          MS  MS
```

Besides carrying the actual voice/data traffic, a few dedicated logical channels handle signaling between the network and mobile terminals: **broadcast channels** carry control information from a base station to every mobile station in its cell, **paging channels** notify a specific mobile station of an incoming call, and **random access channels** are used by mobile stations to initiate a call. The exact names for these channels vary between systems (e.g. LTE uses its own set of physical/logical channel names), but the three underlying purposes — broadcasting control info, paging a specific device, and letting a device request access — recur throughout cellular generations.

### Cell Size Trade-offs

Cell size is a direct trade-off between coverage and capacity. **Bigger cells** happen naturally with lower frequencies (which travel further, see section 3), and mean fewer cells are needed to cover a given area, which lowers infrastructure costs — this suits rural areas with fewer, more spread-out users. The downside is a lower maximum number of users the cell can serve at once, since all of them are sharing the same limited pool of channels. **Smaller cells** flip this trade-off: they support a much higher user density (since the same channels can be reused more densely, closer together), but need more base stations, and therefore cost more to deploy. At the extreme end, **microcells** and **picocells** cover as little as a few streets, a building, or even less, and are used where user density is very high and/or where the signal doesn't travel far anyway (e.g. dense indoor environments).

### Channel Allocation

Once a network's cells are laid out, its available channels still need to be divided among them, and there are three main strategies for this. **Fixed Channel Allocation (FCA)** permanently assigns a fixed set of channels to each cell; it's simple, but wastes spectrum whenever a cell's actual load doesn't match the channels it was given (an idle cell keeps channels doing nothing, while a busy neighboring cell might run short). **Borrowing Channel Allocation (BCA)** patches this by letting heavily loaded cells borrow additional channels from neighbouring cells that are under-loaded. **Dynamic Channel Allocation (DCA)** goes further and keeps all channels in one common pool, with the MSC deciding on the fly which cell gets which channel based on current load — this achieves the best spectrum usage of the three, at the cost of extra complexity and coordination overhead at the MSC.

### User Location

To know where to route an incoming call, a cellular network needs to keep track of roughly where every mobile terminal currently is. Each terminal registers with a specific MSC as its **home area**, and that MSC keeps a **Home Location Register (HLR)**, a database identifying every terminal registered with it and storing information about its current location. When a terminal roams into the coverage of a different MSC, that "hosting" MSC keeps a **Visitor Location Register (VLR)**, a database of terminals that are normally registered elsewhere but are temporarily present in its own area. Since LTE/4G, these functions have been consolidated into other network elements — for instance, the LTE HSS (Home Subscriber Service) aggregates the HLR together with the Authentication Center (AuC), which also strengthens network-terminal security (authentication, encryption).

When a call for a specific terminal comes in, the network checks that terminal's HLR entry at its home MSC. If the terminal is still within its home MSC's coverage, the call is established directly there. If it isn't (i.e. it has roamed elsewhere), the call is redirected to the MSC of the terminal's new location, which checks its own VLR database; if the terminal is found there, that MSC instructs the corresponding base station to page the terminal (notifying it of the incoming call).

### Handoff / Handover

**Handoff** is the process of switching a call from one base station to another during the call itself, triggered by the terminal moving from one cell to another, by a drop in connection quality to the current BS, or both. It's more difficult to implement reliably for terminals crossing cell boundaries at high speed, since the network has to react fast enough to avoid dropping the call — sometimes handled with multilayer cellular networks (microcells nested inside bigger cells) to smooth this out. The decision to hand off can be taken by the MSCs themselves, based on signal measurements reported by the base stations (as in 1st generation systems), or in cooperation with the mobile terminal itself (as in some 2nd generation TDMA systems).

There are two ways to actually carry out the switch. **Soft handoff** ("make-before-break") establishes the link to the new base station before releasing the link to the old one, so the terminal is briefly connected to both at once; this needs the terminal to be capable of talking to more than one BS simultaneously and requires explicit support from the network, making it more complex and expensive, but it maximizes availability and reliability during the switch. **Hard handoff** ("break-before-make") does the opposite: it releases the old link before establishing the new one. It's simpler and cheaper, and is what most cellular systems actually use; it's good enough for telephony and, in most situations, fast enough that it doesn't noticeably affect data applications either, even though in principle it could cause brief losses.
